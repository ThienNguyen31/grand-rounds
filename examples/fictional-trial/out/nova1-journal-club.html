Anti-PD-1 releases the brake that tumour cells put on T cells

The animation loops by itself (16 seconds), so talk over it. Stage 1: the T-cell receptor recognises a tumour antigen presented on MHC. Stage 2: PD-L1 on the tumour binds PD-1 and switches the T cell off. Stage 3: an anti-PD-1 antibody occupies PD-1, so PD-L1 cannot bind. Stage 4: the T cell is released and kills the tumour cell. The vaccine in NOVA-1 aims to add more tumour-specific T cells for anti-PD-1 to release.

Most of the difference is local and flu-like: injection-site pain 48.9 vs 9.1 percent, fever 30.1 vs 6.3. Grade 3 or higher events were similar, 21.4 vs 19.5 percent.

A meaningful early signal, but overall survival is still immature

  1. Recurrence fell: about 12 percentage points at 24 months (HR 0.62).
  2. Survival is not yet answered: HR 0.81 (95% CI 0.52–1.27).
  3. Access depends on tissue: 201 of 1204 screened patients had too little tumour tissue.
Discussion: would you offer this to a stage IIB patient today, given immature survival and the tissue requirement?
